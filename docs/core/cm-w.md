CM-W interprets Diana's training bout against Antiope in Wonder Woman.

It is the longest movement in the [Extended Library](extended-library.md) at thirty-one steps, and the only one that puts unarmed strikes in among the blade work. A back kick, a front kick and a punch are written as cells, on their own beats, in the same rows as the attacks — because the fight it comes from is a training bout between two people who are not only using weapons.

CM-W was notated by Desso and Anna Faulkner of Gulf Coast Saber Training.

## Notation

CM-W is written in four tables, read in order. Each starts its own count, as the source lesson writes them.

Read down each column as usual, and notice how many cells have no blade in them at all.

- **Three cells are unarmed strikes.** `Back Kick` closes Part 2, `Punch` sits at step 6 of Part 3, and `Front Kick` at step 3 of Part 4. Each occupies a full step, and `Punch` is answered by `Deflect` rather than by a parry.
- **`©` is a complete** and **`©A` is a complete across** — carried wide, vertically across — both taught in [CM-C](cm-c-part-2.md) and listed in the [Notation Legend](../notation/legend.md). Every one of them is answered by leaving the line: `Dodge`, `Duck`, `Duck`, `Jump`, `Jump`.
- **The high ones are ducked and the low ones jumped.** `10©A` and `10©` are ducked; `7©A` and `7©` are jumped. The pairing holds every time it appears.
- **The stand-off is pulled apart twice**, at steps 3 and 7 of Part 1 — `Pull left` against `Pull R`, then `Pull right` against `Pull L`, the two Saberists going opposite ways each time, as in [CM-G](cm-g.md).
- **`X` is a lock**, from [CM-E](cm-e.md). `12X` against `12PX` and `3PX` against `3X` are written with the lock in both rows; `2XP` and `10XP` are Saberist A's answers to completes and are written as the source lesson writes them, with the video showing each.
- **`3PM6X` is a modified parry into a lock** — a parry on the 3 line with the tip pointing down at 6, the Legend's `3PM6`, closed into a lock.
- **`Ig` is the inverted grip** taught in [CM-J](cm-j.md). Saberist B takes the last three attacks of the movement in it — `Ig 1`, `Ig 11`, `Ig 12` — and Saberist A answers the first two by dodging.
- **`Spin L>R` and `Spin R>L` are spins**, and there are four, all in Saberist B's row. Two of them, in Part 3, sit on beats where nothing is written for Saberist A.
- **`Special` closes the movement**, in the sense [CM-E](cm-e.md) gives it, and it appears three times in Part 4 — once for Saberist B, then `Special X` and `Special` for Saberist A.
- **Part 2 is two beats for Saberist B alone.** Nothing is written for Saberist A while the spin and the back kick are performed.
- **A blank cell means nothing is written for that Saberist on that beat.**

## Hands and feet

Every movement in the library so far assumes the fight is the blade. Saberists carry sabers, the notation names target lines, and when a cell describes something else — a duck, a spin, a pose — it is describing how a Saberist got out of the way of a blade or set up the next one. [CM-P](cm-p.md) ends on a kick. [CM-R](cm-r.md) has a shove in it. CM-W goes further: it treats a punch as an attack.

Look at step 6 of Part 3. Saberist B's cell says `Punch`. Saberist A's says `Deflect`. That is an attack and an answer, written exactly the way `1` and `1P` are written, on the same beat, in the same table — and neither of them involves a saber. The notation did not need a new symbol for it, and it did not need to explain itself. A punch is a thing one Saberist does to another on a numbered beat, which is all a cell has ever meant.

The two kicks work the same way. `Back Kick` ends Part 2, on a beat where Saberist A has nothing written, so the kick lands into space the choreography has already cleared. `Front Kick` is Saberist A's at step 3 of Part 4, on a beat where Saberist B has nothing written. Both are placed where the other Saberist is not answering, which is the safest possible way to put an unarmed strike into a sequence and is worth noticing as a piece of craft.

That choice reflects the fight the movement comes from. This is a training bout rather than a duel — two people testing each other, where being disarmed or knocked down is a lesson rather than an ending. A notation that could only write blade work would have to leave out half of it.

The blade work that surrounds all this is unusually one-directional at the start. Part 1 opens with Saberist A throwing a complete, an attack, and then two completes across — `11©`, `3`, then `10©A` and `7©A` — and Saberist B answers every one by leaving the line. Dodge, duck, jump. Nothing is parried until the lock at step 6. That is [CM-F](cm-f.md)'s lesson again, performed with a saber in hand against the widest swings in the library.

The choice of answer is not arbitrary, and CM-W is the clearest place in the library to see the rule. `10©A` and `10©` are high, and both are ducked. `7©A` and `7©` are low, and both are jumped. Every complete in the movement follows it. A Saberist who learns that pairing here will read it correctly everywhere else.

Then the movement turns over. Part 3 hands the attack back and forth — `12` against `12P`, then `1` against `1P` — and by Part 4 Saberist B is attacking in an inverted grip while Saberist A dodges: `Ig 1`, `Ig 11`, `Ig 12`. A movement that opened with Saberist A driving forward ends with Saberist A retreating from a reversed blade.

And it ends on special moves. Saberist B takes one at step 5 of Part 4, Saberist A answers the inverted-grip run with `Special X` and then `Special`. Three slots the notation names without filling, at the end of thirty-one steps, in a movement about two people finding out what the other can do. That is the right place for them.

## Purpose

CM-W is designed to teach:

- unarmed strikes — punches and kicks — as written, counted beats
- placing an unarmed strike where the other Saberist is not answering
- answering a complete by its height: ducking the high ones, jumping the low ones
- completes across, and the distance they need
- pulling apart from a stand-off, twice, in both directions
- attacking in an inverted grip against a retreating partner
- holding a thirty-one step movement together across four tables

## Training method

### Phase 1: Completes and their answers

Practise `11©`, `10©A`, `7©A`, `10©` and `7©` with the dodges, ducks and jumps that answer them.

A complete across travels further than anything most Saberists have performed, and there are four completes in Part 1 alone. Set the height and arc of each, agree whether it is ducked or jumped, and rehearse each pair until both Saberists arrive together. None of this is run at speed early.

### Phase 2: Part 1 statically

Walk the ten steps of Part 1 one at a time, with a full stop after each.

Confirm at every step:

- both Saberists agree which target the number names
- the evasion leaves the line the swing is travelling on
- the lock is entered with control rather than collided into
- distance is safe for a swing that does not stop
- the step can be repeated the same way

### Phase 3: The stand-offs and the pulls

Practise `12X` into the pull, and the second pull at step 7, as units.

Enter each bind slowly and by agreement, then break it by pulling in opposite directions on an agreed count. Neither Saberist is trying to win the bind. Set the direction, the count and the distance each travels, and keep them fixed.

### Phase 4: The unarmed strikes

Rehearse `Back Kick`, `Punch` against `Deflect`, and `Front Kick`, each on its own.

These are the beats that need the most agreement and the least speed. For each: agree the target, agree the distance, make no contact, and perform it the same way every repetition. Two of the three land on beats where the other Saberist has nothing written, and that spacing is part of the safety — it is not an invitation to move into the gap.

### Phase 5: Part 3 and the spins

Run Part 3 as a sequence, then work Saberist B's two spins separately.

The spins at steps 7 and 9 fall on beats where nothing is written for Saberist A, so Saberist B is turning while the other Saberist holds. Agree the direction and the spacing before either is run in sequence.

### Phase 6: The inverted-grip run

Practise `Ig 1`, `Ig 11` and `Ig 12` against the two dodges.

Saberist B takes the grip before the run rather than during it, as [CM-J](cm-j.md) sets out, and works all three attacks statically first. Saberist A is dodging rather than parrying for two of the three, so the distance is set from Saberist A's side.

### Phase 7: The specials, then the whole movement

Decide all three special moves before they are practised, then join the four tables at low speed.

Do not improvise the specials during practice. When the whole movement runs, add speed only to the exchanges in Parts 1 and 3 — never to the completes across, the locks, the pulls, the unarmed strikes or the specials.

## Teaching note

CM-W should be taught as a language drill, not as a fight.

Two hazards belong to this movement. The first is the unarmed strikes. A punch and two kicks are the only attacks in the library delivered with a part of the body rather than a blade, and the instinct that keeps a saber at a safe distance does not automatically transfer to a fist or a foot. All three are performed with no contact, at an agreed distance, at a speed that never increases — and the beats around them, where the other Saberist has nothing written, stay empty.

The second is length. Thirty-one steps across four tables is the longest thing in the Extended Library, and the honest way to work it is one table at a time across several sessions. Part 2 is two beats long and easy to lose entirely; it is a real part of the count, and skipping it puts the pair out of step for the whole of Part 3.

It is also worth telling students what this movement settles about the notation. A punch answered by a deflect is written the same way as a `1` answered by a `1P` — same table, same beat, same two rows. Nothing had to be added to the system to hold it. The Saber Standard describes what two Saberists do to each other in sequence, and a saber is one of the things they might be holding while they do it.

CM-W is the last movement in the Extended Library. When it is clean, the whole of that library is behind you — browse the [Community Library](community-library.md) for movements contributed by other schools, or return to the [Extended Library](extended-library.md).
